# Physics — The Derivations List

**Almost every 3- and 5-mark question in the Physics paper is one of the derivations below.** There
are 30 of them. If you can reproduce all 30 on blank paper, with diagrams, you can answer nearly
every long question the board can set.

## How to use this list

**Do not read it.** Reading a derivation is not the same as writing one under time pressure.

1. Keep a **separate derivations notebook** — one derivation per page.
2. Work through the chapter note first, then close it and write the derivation **from memory**, with
   the diagram, on a fresh page. Date it.
3. Revisit each one at **day 7** and **day 30**. Twenty minutes, from memory, no notes.
4. Tick the boxes below as you get each one right **from memory**. Three ticks means you own it.

The **Tier** column tells you what to do first.

| Tier | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **1** | Asked essentially every year. Non-negotiable. Do these first. |
| **2** | Asked most years. Needed for a good score. |
| **3** | Occasional. Do after Tiers 1 and 2 are solid. |

The **Diagram** column matters: where it says **yes**, the diagram carries its own mark and is
awarded independently of the algebra. Draw it even when you cannot finish the derivation.

## The February drill

In the last three weeks, run this once a week. It takes about 90 minutes and it is the highest-return
revision available for Physics.

1. Blank paper, no notes, no formula sheet.
2. Write out all fifteen **Tier 1** derivations, with diagrams. Time yourself: about four minutes
   each.
3. Then all fourteen **graphs**, axes labelled, slopes and intercepts annotated.
4. Score yourself against the chapter notes. Anything you could not produce goes on a single sheet.
5. That sheet — and nothing else — is what you revise the night before.

If you can do this cleanly, the 3- and 5-mark questions in the paper are already answered before you
walk in.
