# How to Answer — question-type playbook

The blueprint tells you what the paper looks like. This tells you what to *write*.

Each question type below has a fixed template. Learn the template and you stop making decisions
under time pressure — which is where marks go.

---

## Universal rules

1. **Formula → substitution → answer.** Three lines, every numerical, both subjects. The middle line
   is where the method marks live.
2. **Draw the diagram, always.** Even when unasked. A ray diagram, circuit, phasor diagram,
   feasible region, or shaded-area sketch carries its own ½–1 mark and is awarded independently.
3. **Units in Physics, arrows on vectors in Maths.** Both cost ½ mark each time.
4. **Never leave a question blank.** Write the formula. Write what is given. Partial method marks are
   real, and Assertion–Reason/MCQs have no negative marking.
5. **Underline or box the final answer**, and state it as a sentence when the question asked in words
   ("Hence the focal length is 20 cm").

---

## Section A — MCQs (1 mark)

**Time: 45 seconds each. Hard limit.**

- Do not solve fully if you can eliminate. Check units, signs, limiting cases, and order of magnitude.
- **Physics dimension trick:** if options differ in units or dimensions, dimensional analysis alone
  usually settles it without any physics.
- **Maths:** substitute a convenient value (x = 0, 1, or π/2) into the given and the options.
- Circle the option letter **and** write the option's content. If the letter is ambiguous the
  examiner reads the content.
- Anything taking over 90 seconds: mark it, move on, come back. Never sink 5 minutes into 1 mark.

## Section A — Assertion–Reason (1 mark)

The standard four options:

- (a) Both A and R true, and R **is** the correct explanation of A
- (b) Both A and R true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A
- (c) A true, R false
- (d) A false, R true

**Method — three separate judgements, in this order.** Do not shortcut it.

1. Is A true, on its own?
2. Is R true, on its own? (R is often a correct textbook statement that is simply irrelevant.)
3. Only if both are true: does R *cause/explain* A?

The trap is almost always at step 3 — two true statements where R explains something else. When both
are true and you are genuinely unsure whether R explains A, **(a) is more often correct than (b)** in
CBSE papers. Use that only as a tiebreak, never as a substitute for step 3.

## Section B — Very Short Answer (2 marks)

**Two marks = two scoring points.** Find them before writing.

- "State X and write its SI unit" → the statement is 1, the unit is 1. Both, explicitly.
- "Define X. Write its dimensional formula." → same structure.
- A 2-mark numerical → formula (1) + substitution and answer with unit (1).
- A 2-mark "why/how" → the reason (1) + the consequence (1).

**Do not over-write.** A 2-mark answer is 2–4 lines. Writing half a page earns nothing extra and
costs you time in Section E. This is the single most common time leak in Physics.

## Section C — Short Answer (3 marks)

**Three marks = three scoring points**, and in Physics one of them is very often the diagram.

Typical splits:
- Physics derivation: diagram (1) + set-up (1) + result (1)
- Physics numerical: formula (1) + substitution (1) + answer with unit (1)
- Maths integral: correct method choice/substitution (1) + integration (1) + simplification and `+C` (1)
- Maths "prove that": start from LHS (1) + key manipulation (1) + reach RHS with a concluding line (1)

Always end a proof with an explicit closing line — "= RHS, hence proved". The examiner looks for it.

## Section D (Physics) / Section E (Maths) — Case-study (4 marks)

- **Read the passage last.** Read the sub-questions first, then mine the passage for exactly the
  numbers you need. Reading the passage first wastes 90 seconds.
- Sub-parts are independent. Getting (i) wrong does not stop you scoring (ii), (iii), (iv) — unless
  you carry a wrong number forward, so recompute rather than reuse when it's cheap.
- The last sub-part usually has an internal choice (OR). Read both, pick fast, **cross out the one
  you abandoned**.
- Answers here are short — one or two lines each. Do not write essays.

## Section E (Physics) / Section D (Maths) — Long Answer (5 marks)

The highest-value questions in the paper, and the most template-driven.

**Physics 5-marker — the fixed order:**
1. **Labelled diagram** (½–1 mark) — ray diagram, circuit, phasor, field lines. First, before any algebra.
2. **State the principle or law** you are using, in words (½ mark).
3. **Set up** the governing equation from that law (1 mark).
4. **Derive** — every algebraic step, no jumps (1½–2 marks).
5. **Box the result**, and state what each symbol means if you introduced new ones (½ mark).
6. **Then** do the numerical or application part, formula → substitution → answer with unit.

If you are stuck on the algebra, write steps 1, 2 and 5 anyway — the diagram, the law, and the known
final result. That is often 2 of 5 marks recovered from a question you could not do.

**Maths 5-marker — the fixed order:**
1. Write what is given, in symbols.
2. Name the method in one line ("Using integration by parts with u = …, dv = …", "By matrix method,
   AX = B so X = A⁻¹B").
3. Full working, one step per line. Never combine two algebraic moves on one line.
4. For area problems: sketch the region and shade it *before* integrating, and write the limits with
   a one-line justification of where they came from (the intersection points).
5. Final answer boxed, with units if the problem had them (cm², cubic units, etc.).

## Subject-specific patterns worth memorising

### Physics: the "derive + numerical" hybrid

Most 5-markers are `derivation (3) + numerical using that result (2)`. Students derive well and then
run out of time on the numerical, or skip the derivation to reach the numerical. Do the derivation
properly — it is the bigger half — and give the numerical its three lines.

### Physics: graph questions

Every graph answer needs **both axes labelled with quantity and unit**, the shape correct, and any
intercept/slope/asymptote marked. Common ones: photoelectric (stopping potential vs frequency;
current vs potential for different intensities), V–I for a diode, impedance vs frequency for LCR,
binding energy per nucleon vs mass number, resistivity vs temperature for conductor/semiconductor.
Learn the *intercept meaning* of each — that is what gets asked.

### Physics: "distinguish between" questions

Answer in a **two-column table**, three points minimum, comparing the *same* attribute on each row.
Comparing different attributes on each side scores badly.

### Maths: integration — choose the method in the first 15 seconds

| If the integrand looks like | Try |
| --- | --- |
| f(g(x))·g'(x) | substitution |
| product of unrelated functions | by parts, ILATE order |
| rational, denominator factorises | partial fractions |
| 1/(quadratic) or 1/√(quadratic) | complete the square → standard form |
| involves √(a²−x²), √(a²+x²), √(x²−a²) | trig substitution x = a sinθ, a tanθ, a secθ |
| definite, limits symmetric about 0 | check odd/even property first |
| definite, limits 0 to a | try the f(x) → f(a−x) property |

Spending 15 seconds classifying beats 5 minutes of a wrong method.

### Maths: Bayes' theorem — the fixed layout

Always write it as: define events → write all priors P(Eᵢ) → write all likelihoods P(A|Eᵢ) → write
the formula → substitute. Laid out that way, you cannot mix up numerator and denominator, which is
the standard way this question is lost.

### Maths: Linear Programming — the fixed layout

1. Tabulate constraints and write them as inequalities, including x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0.
2. Draw the graph on **graph paper**, scale stated, lines labelled with their equations.
3. Shade the feasible region and **name every corner point with its coordinates** (solve the
   simultaneous equations to get them — do not read them off the graph).
4. Table of Z at each corner point.
5. State max/min, at which point, and whether the region is bounded. If unbounded, do the extra
   check (test whether the open half-plane Z > max, or Z < min, has any common point with the
   feasible region) — this is the mark most students lose.

## The last 10 minutes

Do not start a new question. Instead:

1. Check every numerical has a **unit**.
2. Check every vector answer has an **arrow or î ĵ k̂ form**.
3. Check every graph has **labelled axes**.
4. Check you crossed out abandoned internal-choice attempts.
5. Check question numbers match the paper.
6. Check every `∫` in an indefinite integral ended with **`+ C`**.

That checklist is worth 3–5 marks in a typical paper. It is the cheapest revision you will ever do.
